What is logically possible is imaginable. So we can dream of anything, and have indeed dreamed of anything... When we say this, however, “dream” stands metonymically for mental images in general, the images we see when we close our eyes; of the dreams that come to us in sleep, it indicates only a possibility. Yet the problems of reality usually arise within just such restrictions—a restriction, for instance, from mental images in general to dreams we have in sleep. In this context, perhaps reality’s most intractable problem is that changing the order changes the result. Such a problem of order, that is, cannot arise in our mental image of a universe to which we have simply added another dimension of time (or there is the problem of knots that cannot be untied; only recently did I learn that the two are the same).
What I mean is this: however logically possible something may be, it does not always appear before our eyes with concrete features. At the same time, the dreams we have in sleep are concrete enough (on the extent to which dreams and reality can be confused, see R. Descartes (1641)).

The meaning of a dream one wants to dream is now clear. Such a dream concerns things whose concrete forms one wants to witness vividly—or, rather, must witness—even though there is no way for them to come about through physical phenomena. That this naturally becomes a part of visual art deserves attention. Meanwhile, without restricting “otaku” to yumejoshi (夢女子), I can still write: there is the fact that every otaku has at least one dream they want to dream. This follows from our being able to understand every otaku, in the broad sense, as a creator of derivative works. Here, the dream one wants to dream is visual art, a derivative work of reality.

Yet to liken an otaku who has a dream they want to dream to an artist in general obscures what is at stake. Such an analogy is, as before, a burial: it covers over the dream’s character as mourning. The point is that a dream one wants to dream is, in one respect, a natural extension of the state of mourning. After all, someone who must mourn has a dream they want to dream, and someone who has a dream they want to dream bears the ache of “failed” mourning—in which “the dead person (...) continues to live within me.” In this sense, beyond the analogy with the artist, the otaku has also been a mourner.

Dreaming the dream one wants to dream, or stepping outside that dream, is therefore the fulfilment of romantic longing and the most personal act of derivative creation, while also being the “success” of mourning. Yet this stepping outside is, in reality, nothing. For a dream one wants to dream is a dream doubled.
Having a dream one wants to dream differs from having a dream. The dream in “having a dream” is directed toward reality: a dream that this or that might really happen. Having a dream one wants to dream, on the other hand, is a phenomenon in which the very act of dreaming a dream has itself become a dream, and its fulfilment has become a matter of chance, independent of the dreamer’s will or actions. Even if this doubled dream comes true, it is no result of the dreamer’s unceasing efforts. It leaves only a cloud of meaninglessness, as though the dream had come true without anything in the world having changed. A dream is certainly concrete; indeed, precisely because it is concrete, the boundary between absence and presence is clear—a sharply defined front of intense meteorological activity. In this sense, the boundary of a dream one wants to dream is meaninglessness.

Dreaming the dream one wants to dream brings that dream to an end, insofar as it is the “success” of mourning. And this ending, this ending that looks so much like salvation, is nothing other than meaninglessness. Once outside the dream one wants to dream, then, one can no longer remain an otaku. What is more, although mourning for the content of the dream has “succeeded,” melancholy changes direction and persists: because the outcome of the dream one wanted to dream is meaninglessness, one dwells once more on its reverberations. The ending of the dream one wants to dream resolves nothing at all; it merely castrates the dream. The dream has ended, and so, when time has passed and the clouds have cleared, only then will its content no longer remain.
This natural forgetting, which might also be called a cure, is the killing of derivative creation, a graduation beyond graduation. All that remains is a boundary that will appear from time to time.

At the end of this analysis, then, stands an injunction: the dream one wants to dream must not be dreamed. If there is such a quality as inevitably coming to pass, it is no longer a hopeful one. Unless they come to hate being an otaku, an otaku wants to remain an otaku for life. Producing a new dream each time a dream ends does not solve the problem. Thus the dream one wants to dream must not be dreamed. To break free of this inevitable double bind, one must realise the dream one wants to dream without dreaming it. It follows that the otaku is in a position where becoming a creator of derivative works—in the narrow sense—is unavoidable. My final claim, then, is this: the double bind surrounding the dream an otaku wants to dream is the fundamental problem of the otaku.
